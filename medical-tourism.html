Can healthcare become a destination?

I explored whether Saudi Arabia has the data signals to compete as a regional medical-tourism hub—connecting patient care, cost patterns and future demand to a strategic question.

The question wasn’t “which model wins?” — it was what does the data say Saudi Arabia should do next?

01 / The business question

From hospital data to national opportunity.

The analysis asked three practical questions: whether treatment costs and clinical outcomes differ meaningfully between Saudi and non-Saudi patients; whether future patient flow can be predicted; and which factors most influence treatment costs.

That framing turned a healthcare dataset into a strategy case: is the patient experience consistent enough, and is demand strong enough, to support medical tourism?

02 / The data

79,000 patient journeys.

The dataset covered 63,046 Saudi and 15,954 non-Saudi patients across Riyadh, Jeddah and Madinah, with demographic, clinical and financial variables including age, severity, length of stay, comorbidities, lab tests, diagnosis, radiology procedures and total treatment cost.

Missing values were imputed, numeric features standardized, categorical fields encoded, and SMOTE was applied only to training data to address class imbalance.

One question. Multiple lenses.

05 / A useful failure

The cost models were not the headline.

Cost-segmentation accuracy stayed around 24–26% with F1 near 0.25. Regression models also produced R² values close to zero with average MAE around SAR 15,900.

Rather than hiding weak predictive performance, the case study treats it as information: individual healthcare costs remained highly heterogeneous and were not well explained by the available variables.

06 / Where the signal became strategic

Demand points upward.

Patient-inflow models used time-aware validation with lag and trend features. Reported validation showed R² of roughly .97–1.00 and MAPE around 3%.

The project’s scenario projections indicate continued growth in international patient volume through 2030, turning forecasting into a capacity-planning and market-development question rather than just a modeling exercise.

08 / What I’d improve next

Strong analysis also knows its limits.

The source study notes limitations including public-hospital coverage, concentration on three cities, possible synthetic patterns introduced by SMOTE, reliance on historical data, and the absence of patient-satisfaction measures. A next iteration would broaden geographic/private-sector coverage and add patient-experience and policy variables.
